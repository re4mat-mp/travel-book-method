Day 1 · Kanazawa

A city that kept its gold

金箔 · kinpaku · gold leaf

A castle town on the Sea of Japan side that was never bombed in the war, so the old streets are still the old streets. Today is a garden, a teahouse district and a market.

A garden with six virtues

兼六園 · Kenroku-en · the garden that combines six is counted among the three great gardens of Japan. It began as the outer garden of the castle and grew over almost two centuries, one lord after another adding a pond, a hill, a stream.

The name comes from an old Chinese text about gardens, which says a garden can rarely have six qualities at once, because some of them pull against each other: space and seclusion, human work and age, running water and wide views. This one was meant to have all six. As you walk, try to find the place where the garden feels enclosed and, a few steps later, the place where it suddenly opens.

If you come in the cold months, look up at the pines. From late autumn, gardeners tie ropes from a pole at the top of each tree down to its branches, like the ribs of an umbrella. It is called 雪吊り · yukitsuri · snow hanging: the ropes carry the weight of the wet snow so the branches do not snap.

The teahouse street

ひがし茶屋街 · Higashi Chaya-gai · the eastern teahouse district was laid out by the domain in the early nineteenth century as a place for entertainment, with music, dance and conversation. The two-storey wooden fronts are still there, with fine vertical slats on the ground floor that let the people inside see out while the street cannot see in.

Some of the houses are still working teahouses; others are shops and cafés. Walk it twice if you can: once in daylight for the wood, and once at dusk, when the lamps come on and the street goes quiet.

Gold you can eat

Almost all of the gold leaf made in Japan comes from Kanazawa. A craftsman beats a small piece of gold between sheets of special paper until it is around a ten-thousandth of a millimetre thick, so thin that light passes through it. It covers temple altars and lacquer boxes, and, because it has no taste at all, it ends up on ice cream too.

One to tell at the table: the city's own story is that the damp air of this coast helped the craft settle here: thin gold and paper are said to behave better when the air is humid, and Kanazawa has plenty of it.

The market

近江町市場 · Ōmichō ichiba · the Ōmichō market has fed the city for around three hundred years. In winter, look for the crabs from this coast, laid out in rows by size. Many carry a small plastic tag on one leg: it says which coast and which port the crab came from, the way a label does on a bottle of wine.

What to look at today

The ropes on the pines, the slats on the teahouse fronts, and the gold leaf workshops' windows, where you can sometimes watch a sheet being lifted with bamboo tweezers.
