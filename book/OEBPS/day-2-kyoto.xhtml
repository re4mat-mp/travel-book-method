Day 2 · Kyoto

Fifteen stones, never all at once

枯山水 · karesansui · a dry landscape: water drawn with gravel

A garden you are not allowed to walk in, a curtain that tells you whether a shop is open, and a market that has been the city's kitchen for centuries.

A garden made of gravel

龍安寺 · Ryōan-ji · the temple of the peaceful dragon is a Zen temple in the north-west of the city, and part of the group of Kyoto monuments on the World Heritage list. Its fame is one rectangle of white gravel behind a low clay wall, with fifteen stones set in five groups.

There are no plants in it except the moss around the stones. The gravel is raked in straight lines, and in rings around each group, so that it reads as water around islands. Nobody knows for sure who made it, or exactly when, and nobody agrees on what it means. Some see a tiger carrying her cubs across a river; others, mountains above the clouds. The temple leaves the question open.

Sit on the wooden veranda and count the stones. From wherever you sit, one is always hidden behind another: you will see fourteen at most. Move along the veranda and a different one disappears.

Behind the hall

Walk round the back of the main hall and look for a small stone basin, where water runs into a square hollow. Around the square are four characters that only make sense when the square in the middle is read as part of each one. Together they say 吾唯足知 · ware tada taru o shiru · I only know contentment. It is a Zen idea: having enough is already a kind of wealth.

One to tell at the table: several guides say the basin visitors see is a copy, and that the old one stands in a part of the temple that is not open to the public.

The city's kitchen

錦市場 · Nishiki ichiba · the Nishiki market is a single covered street, narrow and long, lined with stalls of pickles, tofu, fish and knives. People in the city call it 京の台所 · kyō no daidokoro · Kyoto's kitchen. Some stalls have been run by the same family for generations.

Before you go in, look at the doorways. Most shops hang a short split curtain over the entrance, the 暖簾 · noren. A curtain hanging outside means come in. No curtain means the shop has finished for the day, whatever else the front seems to say. The curtain is also the shop's sign: its name, its crest, sometimes the year it was founded.

What to look at today

The stone you cannot see, the words around the basin, and the curtains: how many of them carry a date, and how old the oldest one is.
